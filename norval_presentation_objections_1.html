Technical Objections

Undervaluation, conditional vs unconditional, depreciation questions, rental yield concerns. These require clear, confident explanations — not waffling. Know these cold before training with Haydn.

"What is an undervaluation? What happens if the bank values it for less than purchase price?"
Technical
▼
Haydn asked you this — know it cold. An undervaluation occurs when the bank's independent valuer assesses the property at a lower figure than the contracted purchase price. This affects how much the bank will lend.
Great question — and it's something worth understanding before you sign anything.

An undervaluation is when the bank sends out their own independent valuer, and that valuer comes back with a figure lower than what you've agreed to pay. For example — you contract to purchase a property for $750,000, but the bank's valuer assesses it at $700,000. The bank will only lend against the lower figure.

What that means practically is: instead of borrowing 80% of $750,000 ($600,000), you'd only be able to borrow 80% of $700,000 ($560,000). You'd need to make up the $40,000 gap from your own funds — either from cash, equity, or you'd need to pay lenders mortgage insurance to bridge the shortfall.

Now — this is less common on new construction than on established, for a simple reason. On new builds, the bank is valuing a contract price, not a comparable sale. And because we source properties at intrinsic cost — land plus build, no retail premium — the valuation is less likely to come in under because there's no inflated margin built into the price to begin with.

That said, it can happen in fast-moving markets where comparable sales haven't caught up yet. If it does happen, our operations team will work through the options with you — it's not a deal-breaker, it just needs a conversation.
What Haydn is really testing: Do you understand the finance mechanics well enough to explain them confidently to a client? Know this answer cold — and know that undervaluations are more likely on established properties where there's a retail premium baked into the price.
"What's the difference between a conditional and unconditional contract?"
Technical
▼
Another Haydn training question — know this cold.
Conditional contract: The purchase is subject to one or more conditions being satisfied before it becomes binding. The most common conditions are finance approval (subject to the bank formally approving your loan) and building and pest inspection (subject to a satisfactory inspection of the property).

While those conditions are live, either party has the ability to exit the contract without penalty if the condition isn't met. For example — if your finance is declined, you can exit a conditional contract and get your deposit back. The contract only becomes fully binding once all conditions are waived or satisfied.

Unconditional contract: There are no outstanding conditions. Both parties are fully committed. Once a contract goes unconditional, you cannot exit without losing your deposit and potentially facing legal action for breach of contract.

In the context of new construction — your initial expression of interest is effectively conditional, because finance isn't formally approved yet. The contract typically becomes unconditional once the bank has formally issued approval, the cooling-off period has passed, and any conditions have been waived. Our operations team walks you through each of those milestones so you're never in the dark.
Key point to remember: The EOI tonight is not unconditional. It's an expression of interest — it takes the property off the market while finance is arranged. The client is not locked in without an out at this stage.

TechnicalEducation

Yield is only one part of the return equation — and chasing yield at the expense of capital growth is one of the most common mistakes first-time investors make.

High yield areas typically offer that yield because growth is low. The market is pricing in low demand for the asset — so to attract tenants and investors, the rent has to be proportionally high. What you end up with is an income-producing asset that doesn't grow.

What we're focused on is total return. A property growing at 8 to 10 percent per annum on a $700,000 asset is generating $56,000 to $70,000 in capital growth per year. No high-yield property in a flat-growth market is going to outperform that. The rent is there to cover holding costs — and that's exactly what it does here. The growth is where the wealth is built.

The properties that offer 6 and 7 percent yields are typically in regional areas with limited infrastructure, limited population growth, and limited resale demand. That's not where we operate.
"How does the depreciation and tax return actually work?"
TechnicalEducation
▼
Depreciation is essentially this — even though your property is going up in value in the real world, on paper the carpets are getting older, the blinds are getting older, the appliances are getting older. A quantity surveyor puts a dollar figure on that theoretical wear each year, and you can claim that against your taxable income.

What makes new construction so powerful is the method you're allowed to use. On a new build, you can claim on the structure AND everything in it — using the diminishing value method, which front-loads most of the depreciation into the first 5 to 7 years. That's when you need the tax benefit most, to help cover holding costs while the property is growing.

On an established property under the 2026 budget rules — you get zero negative gearing benefit. None. The entire depreciation advantage disappears.

In practical terms — on the property you're looking at tonight, the combination of rental income, depreciation, and the interest deduction reduces what this property actually costs you out of pocket per week to the figure on that cash flow page. That's not an estimate — that's the structure working exactly as designed.

Finance IO vs P&I — Interest Only vs Principal & Interest
▼

Interest Only (IO): You pay only the interest on the loan each month. The principal (the loan amount) doesn't reduce. Lower monthly repayment. Used on investment properties to maximise cash flow and keep the interest deduction high.

Principal & Interest (P&I): You pay both interest and a portion of the principal each month. Higher repayments but the loan reduces over time. Typically used on owner-occupied loans where you want to pay it off.

Why IO is used for investment properties: Keeps the loan high (and therefore the interest deduction high for tax purposes). Also keeps repayments lower, improving cash flow. The property's capital growth is doing the wealth-building work — not the principal reduction.

ATO PAYG Variation: Rather than waiting until tax time for your full return, you can lodge a PAYG withholding variation with the ATO. This instructs your employer to reduce the tax withheld from your pay throughout the year — meaning you get the benefit weekly, not in a lump sum at the end.

Strategy Delayed Registration — The Full Explanation
▼

What it is: Locking in today's pricing on a property that won't register (receive council approval for construction to commence) until a future date — typically 6 to 18 months away.

How it works: Expression of interest placed on a future land release stage. Fixed-price land paperwork and build contract locked in at today's pricing. Any value growth between now and registration belongs to the buyer — before construction starts and before a single mortgage repayment is made.

Real example: The Robinsons — Deebing Heights, Ipswich. Held mid-2025 at ~$820K. Just registered. Now valued at $1.05M+. $170K in equity before breaking ground.

Who it's for: Conditional-based investors — clients with a condition that needs to be met before they can fully commit (sale settlement, DVA payout, end of posting). Delayed registration gives them time AND today's price.
