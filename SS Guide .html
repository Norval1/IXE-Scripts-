Station 1 The Two Sides of the Property Equation
▼
COL What he does & says
Col frames every client's buying power as one equation — two sides, both need to work.

"There are two sides to the property equation. On the left side we've got deposit funds, and on the right side we have borrowing capacity. There's no use having one without the other."
Left side — Deposit funding checklist (in order):
  • Cash (obvious)
  • Equity (but always checks if it's actually usable)
  • Defence / civilian entitlements
  • Superannuation (planted as a future seed, not covered now)
Equity caveat: Only usable if you're on the title and the property can service more debt. If a spouse's property and they're not working — it's stuck equity. Solution: sell and redeploy as cash.

Right side — Investment vs owner-occupier lending:
For a home to live in — banks assess surplus income only.
For an investment property — banks also count the property's future rental income.

"For a home to live in, what they use to determine how much they'll lend is your surplus income. For an investment property, they also take the future rental income of the property you're looking to acquire into consideration."
This means investment borrowing capacity is often higher than home-buyer borrowing capacity for the same person.
NORVAL How I'd say it
"When we look at whether you can actually do this, there are two things we need to have in place — deposit funds on one side, and your borrowing capacity on the other. No point having one without the other."
Walk through their deposit sources one by one — cash, equity, entitlements.

On equity — always check if it's actually deployable before assuming it is:
"The equity question is important — it's only usable if your name is on the title and the bank will let you borrow against it. If it's sitting in a property under someone else's name, it can't actually do anything for you."
On investment vs owner-occupier lending — this is a huge reframe, use it every time:
"Here's something most people don't realise — when the bank assesses you for an investment property, they don't just look at your income. They also count the rent the property's going to bring in. Which means your borrowing capacity for an investment property is often higher than it would be for a home to live in. That surprises a lot of people."
Why this matters: When a client says "I can't afford a house," they might actually be able to afford an investment property. Flip the conversation — investing first is often the path that's actually available sooner.
ADF Defence Entitlements — Don't Cash In Early
▼
COL What he does & says
Col runs a worked comparison to show why cashing in entitlements for the FHOG is a bad trade.

DHA subsidised rent: client paying $600/week market rent, but DHA means they pay $600/fortnight. That's $300/week saved = $15,600/year.

Queensland FHOG = $30,000 lump sum.

Two years of continued service = $31,200 in rental assistance. Already worth more than the grant.

"It is certainly not the appropriate time to do that... you'd be forfeiting access to rental assistance, and you might as well destroy your ability to invest."
He also killed the idea of gaming the FHOG by separating from a spouse temporarily — cleanly, without moralising, just by running the real numbers and showing it wasn't worth it.
NORVAL How I'd say it
This one I know personally — veteran to veteran:

"Look, I get why the first home owner grant sounds appealing — $30,000 looks like free money. But here's the reality. If you're still in uniform and you've got access to DHA accommodation, that rental assistance is worth around $15,000 a year to you. Two years in and you've already outperformed the grant — and that's before we even talk about the opportunity cost of what that deposit could do in an investment property instead."
"The entitlements aren't a bonus — they're a serious financial asset. We need to make sure we use them at the right time, not just cash them out for a one-off payment."
Bottom line to remember: Ongoing benefit always beats a one-off lump sum if the timeline makes sense. Run the numbers, don't just assume the grant is worth taking.
Station 2 Bonds vs Shares vs Property — The Leverage Comparison
▼
COL What he does & says

Same $200K, same 10% return: $200K in bonds → $20K growth
$200K as deposit on $800K property → $80K growth
Same money, same rate — 4x more in property because of leverage.
"It's the same 200K. It's the same 10% return, but because it's parked in a property, you make 400% more — four times as much — because of access to leverage."
On shares — the critique is volatility and concentration risk, named specifically:
• 90% of S&P 500 weighting in 7 tech companies
• US adding $100B to debt every 100 days
• Geopolitical exposure — US/China, Iran, Ukraine, tariffs

"I love stocks and stocks can be lucrative, but when I have a look at where I want my family's money parked... I would rather not be invested into a volatile asset class if there was a safe asset class sitting somewhere else."
NORVAL How I'd say it
Don't argue property vs other assets in general terms — run the numbers:

"Let me show you something that surprises most people. If you took $200,000 and got a 10% return in bonds, you'd make $20,000. But if you took that same $200,000 and used it as a deposit on an $800,000 property — at the same 10% growth — you'd make $80,000. Same money, same return rate, four times the result. That's what leverage does."
On shares — be balanced, don't trash them, just name the current risks:

"I'm not saying shares are bad — I invest in them myself. But right now, 90% of the S&P 500's weighting sits in seven tech companies. Add in what's happening geopolitically and with US debt — it's a volatile place to park your family's money when there's a safer asset class available."
Key move: Always run an actual dollar comparison with the SAME number through both scenarios. Never argue in percentages alone — it's too abstract. Make the leverage gap visible arithmetic.
Budget Negative Gearing — Historical Precedent Over Opinion
▼
COL What he does & says
Col never argues the politics — he reaches for historical precedent and a plain supply mechanism.

The mechanism in one breath:
"If you disincentivise investors from owning rental stock... you've decreased the supply of rentals. And then you've got the same amount of people that need them, which means there's more competition for the existing rental stock, and therefore the rental price is increased."
Historical precedent:
• 1980s Australia — abolished negative gearing, rents rose sharply within 18 months, reversed after ~2 years
• New Zealand — same policy experiment, same result, reversed after ~2 years
• Today's vacancy rates (0.3–1.8%) are half what they were in the 80s — same policy causes more damage now

Apple tree analogy:
"If I've got an apple tree that can grow 5 apples a year, and 10 people want those 5 apples, and now 50 people want those 5 apples — I haven't created any more apples."
Funnelling investors toward new construction only doesn't fix supply — it concentrates more competition onto the same limited build volume.
NORVAL How I'd say it
When anyone brings up the budget as a reason to hesitate — go straight to the historical precedent:

"Here's the thing — this has actually happened before. We abolished negative gearing in the 1980s. Within 18 months, rents went up so sharply they had to reverse the decision two years later. New Zealand did the exact same thing and reversed it two years after. That's not my opinion — that's documented history."
Then the supply mechanism — keep it simple:

"And here's the part most people miss — taking negative gearing off established properties and pushing investors toward new construction doesn't actually create more homes. If the industry can only build 160,000 homes a year, and you double the demand for those homes, you've still only got 160,000 homes. The apple tree doesn't grow more apples just because more people want them."
The real close: Budget changes actually create more urgency to act on new construction now — because they funnel all investor demand into the exact properties we source, driving more competition and more growth on those assets.

Offset The Offset Account — Net vs Gross Return Argument
▼
COL The full explanation
This is one of Col's most rehearsed pieces — he uses a near-identical version in multiple sessions. The core insight: money in an offset earns you your mortgage rate tax-free, which is worth more than a higher gross return elsewhere after tax.

The comparison — $100K, where does it go? Option A: Stocks at 8% gross → after tax (~30%), net ≈ 5.6%
Option B: Offset account at 6.5% → net 6.5% (tax-free)
To earn the same spendable 6.5% from stocks, you'd need ~10–11% gross return
"The $6,500 that you are saving in the offset account is actually earning you the equivalent of 10 to 11% if you had it in stocks... you don't have to pay tax on money that you've saved inside an offset account."
The other key point: money in the offset is still yours. It's liquid. It's not gone — it's just parked in the most tax-efficient position available.

"We'd rather keep our money in our pocket than give it to Westpac."
NORVAL How I'd say it
This is a brilliant reframe — most people have never had it explained this way:

"Here's something that catches a lot of people off guard. If you've got cash sitting somewhere, and you compare putting it in the offset account versus stocks — most people look at the numbers and think stocks are better because the return looks higher. But that's not comparing apples to apples."
"If your mortgage rate is 6.5%, and you put money in the offset — you're saving 6.5% in interest, and that saving is completely tax-free. To get the same spendable outcome from shares, you'd need to earn around 10 or 11% gross, because you'd have to pay tax on whatever you made before you could actually spend it. So the offset is often the better place for your cash — and the money is still yours, still liquid, still there if you need it."
The punchline: Offset = tax-free 6.5%. Shares = need 10–11% gross to match it. Most clients have never heard this and it genuinely surprises them.
Strategy Delayed Registration — How It Works & Why It Matters
▼
COL The full explanation
Col introduces delayed registration as the solution for "conditional-based investors" — clients who need a condition met (sale of property, DVA payout, etc.) before they can fully commit.

The developer staging mechanism:
"If you're a developer and you have 100 blocks, you're not going to release all 100 at once... they release in stages — 40, 50, 100 lots at a time — so that demand always slightly outpaces the supply they've chosen to release."
Two reasons developers stage:
1. Manufactured scarcity — 1,000 people fighting over 20 blocks instead of 100
2. Manufactured equity — each stage priced higher than the last

The strategy: Put an expression of interest in on a future stage now, at today's pricing. Lock in July 2026 prices for a property that doesn't register until March 2027. Any growth between now and then belongs to you — before you've laid a single brick or made a repayment.

Real example — The Robinsons (Deebing Heights):
Put property on hold mid-2025 for ~$820K. Land just registered. Property now valued at $1.05M+. $170K in equity — without breaking ground yet.
NORVAL How I'd say it
Lead with naming the client's situation before explaining the solution:

"So here's where your situation is interesting — you've got everything in place, but you've got a condition that needs to be met first before you can pull the trigger completely. That's actually pretty common, and there's a strategy built specifically for it."
Then explain delayed registration simply:

"Developers release land in stages rather than all at once — they do it deliberately to keep demand ahead of supply and push prices up with each release. What we can do is get in on a future stage now, at today's price. So by the time that land registers — say six to nine months from now — any growth that's happened belongs to you, even though you haven't started building yet and haven't made a single repayment."
Then the real numbers:

"We had clients put a property on hold in Deebing Heights in mid-2025 for around $820,000. The land just registered. It's now worth over a million. They made $170,000 in equity before the build even started. That's delayed registration working exactly as it should."
The key objection to pre-empt: "What if the builder goes bust while we're waiting?" Answer: we only work with vetted boutique builders with strong balance sheets — not volume builders. And we always have a second builder lined up as contingency. We've never had to use it.

Difference 1 Stamp Duty — The $40K Head Start
▼
COL What he says
Definition first — always:
"The definition of an established property is that it's one second old when you purchase it... even if it's a brand-new build that somebody else has built, and you have purchased it from them, then it's considered established in the eyes of the ATO."
On $1M properties:
New build — duty on land component only ≈ $15,000
Established — duty on full purchase price ≈ $45,000–$55,000
Gap = $30,000–$40,000

"That extra $40K in stamp duty doesn't go towards the property, doesn't go towards the loan — it's just money you're pissing away to the state revenue office."
Col then immediately downplays the stamp duty point to set up the depreciation argument as the bigger deal — "I don't mean to sound like a wanker, but $40K isn't the world's biggest deal in property investing... the points I'm about to share are even more compelling."
NORVAL How I'd say it
Lead with the definition — it surprises people:

"Before I go into the comparison, the definition of established is important. It doesn't mean old. An established property is literally anything someone else has bought before you — it could be brand new, built six months ago. The moment it's changed hands, the ATO considers it established. That matters for what I'm about to show you."
Then the stamp duty gap:

"On a million-dollar new build, you're paying roughly $15,000 in stamp duty — because you're only paying duty on the land. On a million-dollar established property, you're looking at $45,000 to $55,000 — on the full price, at a higher rate because you're an investor. That's up to $40,000 gone before you've done anything — and it doesn't go to the property, it doesn't reduce your loan. It's just gone."
Then set up the next point:

"And honestly, the stamp duty gap is just the start. What I'm about to show you next is where the real difference is."
Difference 2 Depreciation — The Six-Figure Gap
▼
COL The full breakdown
Two methods to know cold:
Prime cost — established only. Structure only, no fixtures/fittings. Flat 2.5% per year over 40 years.
Diminishing value — new builds only. Structure AND plant/equipment. Front-loads the bulk of claims into first 5–7 years, then tapers.

Why the front-loading matters:
"The time when you need, and want, the most tax benefit is in the first 7 years of ownership. That's when you want the tax returns to be so large they're actually helping you cover the holding costs."
After 7 years, the rent has typically grown 5–7x in the right area — so rent takes over from where the tax benefit tapers off.

2026 budget rule change:
"Based on the new budget, you cannot negatively gear an established property at all anymore, even if you bought it when it was one minute old. So you now get zero tax benefit back in that financial year for the established property."
NORVAL How I'd say it
Explain depreciation simply first, then the gap:

"Depreciation is basically this — even though your property is going up in value in the real world, on paper the carpets, the blinds, the appliances are getting older. A quantity surveyor puts a dollar figure on that theoretical wear, and you can claim it on your tax return. You're reducing your taxable income without actually spending anything. That's powerful."
Then the difference between new and established:

"On a new build, you can claim on the structure and everything in it — the full fit-out — using a method that front-loads most of the claim into the first five to seven years, when you need it most to help cover holding costs. On an established property, under the new budget rules, you get zero — you can't negatively gear it at all anymore."
The punchline:

"Over the first seven years of ownership, the difference in claimable tax deductions between a new build and an established property next door — identical in every other way — is over $100,000. That's not the stamp duty. That's the depreciation gap alone."

IXE Difference Why We Don't Do House-and-Land Packages

You're paying whatever they've priced it at, with no competition and no flexibility. We do it differently — we find the best block for your strategy first, then bring our panel of vetted builders in and let them compete on price and inclusions. That means you're paying the actual cost of the land plus the actual cost of the build — not someone else's profit margin on top."
Then the accountability argument:

"And here's something that gives a lot of people comfort — the builders we work with aren't just accountable to you as one family. We send some of them 50 clients a year. At around $450,000 a build, that's over $20 million in revenue for them annually. If they did the wrong thing by you, they'd be putting that entire relationship at risk. The commercial incentive to look after you properly is enormous."

Habit 1 Meta-Narration — He Tells You What He's Doing While He's Doing It
▼
COL The habit
Col constantly narrates the shape of the conversation as it unfolds. "So what I'm going to do now is..." / "This next bit is going to feel a bit maths-heavy, bear with me..." / "So there's the first station on the train track — the next station is..."

This keeps the client oriented and makes complex material feel guided rather than dumped on them.
NORVAL How to use it
"Okay, so I've just covered the deposit side — now I want to show you the borrowing capacity side, and this is where it gets interesting for your situation specifically."
"This next part is a bit number-heavy but stick with me — it's the most important part."
Habit 2 The Verdict Habit — Answer First, Explain Second
▼
COL The habit
Col never leaves a station open-ended. He asks the question plainly, answers it in one word or a short phrase, pauses, and only then explains. "Can you do this logistically? Yes." The pause after the one-word answer is doing all the work — it reads as confident and decisive.
NORVAL How to use it
Before any explanation, state the verdict first:
"Can you do this? Yes. [pause] Here's why..."
"Is now the right time? Yes. [pause] And here's what the data shows..."
"Should you do new or established? New. [pause] Let me show you the numbers."
Habit 3 Analogy First — Always Before the Numbers
▼
COL The habit
Every abstract concept gets tied to a plain everyday analogy before the numbers are shown. Apple tree (supply). Car warranty (build quality). Offset account vs savings account (keeping money in your pocket). Analogy → numbers → verdict. Every time.
NORVAL My analogies to use
Leverage: "Same $200K, same 10% return — but in property you make four times more. That's the power of leverage."
Supply: "You can't grow more apples just because more people want them."
Build quality: "Great Wall vs Toyota warranty — you know immediately which one is better built."
Offset: "Why give the money to the bank when it can stay in your pocket and do the same job?"
Habit 4 Pre-Empt Objections — State Them Yourself Before They're Raised
▼
COL The habit
Col almost never waits for an objection to be raised. He states it himself, out loud, before the client can — which strips it of its power and makes him look transparent rather than defensive. "Now, some people hear this and say... and here's why that's not actually the issue." The pattern: name the concern, answer with a worked number or historical precedent.
NORVAL How to use it
Before they can object — name it yourself:
"Now, a lot of people hear 'new construction' and immediately think — what if I have to wait six months for it to be tenanted? Fair question. Here's the reality..."
"I know what you're probably thinking — what about the builder going bust while we're waiting? That's a reasonable concern. Here's how we handle it..."
Habit 5 Personal Skin-in-the-Game — He References His Own Portfolio
▼
COL The habit
Rather than citing abstract statistics, Col repeatedly references his own portfolio, his own decisions, and his family's financial choices as lived evidence. "On my established portfolio, I've poured $100K into repairs in the last 12 months. On my new-build portfolio — smoke alarm tests and pest control." This is far more credible than third-party testimonials because he's demonstrably not asking clients to do anything he hasn't already done himself.
NORVAL How to use it
Use your own journey — veteran to veteran:
"I'm on the same path myself — building my own portfolio the same way I'm showing you. I'm not asking you to do anything I'm not doing."
And use the Robinsons case study (Deebing Heights) as your go-to real example:
"We had clients put a property on hold in Deebing Heights in mid-2025 for around $820K. Land just registered. It's now worth over a million. $170K in equity before they broke ground."
Habit 6 Always End With One Concrete Next Step + Timeframe
▼
COL The habit
Every session ends with a specific, named next action and a specific timeframe. Never "let's stay in touch." Never "think about it and get back to me." Always a concrete commitment — "I'll send you the introduction today" / "Let's book Friday the 17th" / "Once you've chatted to Lana, let me know and we'll get moving."
NORVAL How to use it
Never leave a session without a named next step:
"So here's what happens next — I'm going to send you the webinar link today. Watch it with your partner if you can. Then let's lock in a time this week or next for the follow-up session. What days work for you?"
"Before we finish — action items from my end: I'll get you the referral to [accountant/DVA] by end of day. From your end, just have a think about what we covered and jot down any questions. Then we'll pick this up on [day]."
