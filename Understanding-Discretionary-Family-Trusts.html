Section 01

What Is a Trust? — The Basics

A trust is a legal arrangement where one party (the trustee) holds and manages assets on behalf of another party (the beneficiaries). It's not a company. It's not a person. It's a relationship — and that relationship is what gives it power.

Think of a trust like a safe-deposit box that someone holds the keys to on your behalf. The person holding the keys (the trustee) doesn't own what's inside in any meaningful sense — they're legally obligated to look after it for someone else. That "someone else" is the beneficiary.

The Three Certainties

For a trust to be legally valid in Australia, three things must be clear from day one. If any one of these is missing, the trust fails.

1

Certainty of Intention

It must be obvious that the person setting up the trust genuinely intended to create one. Vague gifts or loose "I'll hold this for you" arrangements aren't enough — there must be a clear intention to bind the trustee to fiduciary duties.

2

Certainty of Subject Matter

The assets in the trust must be clearly identifiable. "Some of my shares" is not certain. "All 1,000 of my BHP shares" is. The trust property has to be defined with enough precision that everyone knows what's in and what's out.

3

Certainty of Objects

The beneficiaries must be identifiable. They don't all have to be named — a class like "the children of James Mitchell" is fine — but it has to be possible to say with certainty whether any given person falls inside or outside the class.

Legal vs Beneficial Ownership

🏠
An everyday analogy Imagine James Mitchell holds the house deed in his name, but the house was bought with his daughter's inheritance and is meant for her when she turns 25. James is the legal owner — his name's on the title, he can sign documents, he handles the rates. But his daughter is the beneficial owner — she's the one who actually benefits from the property, and James is legally bound to look after it for her. That split between "whose name is on the paperwork" and "who actually benefits" is the heart of every trust.
Section 02

The Key Roles

Every discretionary family trust has four roles. Understanding who does what — and crucially, where the real power sits — is the single most important thing to grasp before you set one up.

Role 1

Settlor

An independent third party (often the lawyer or accountant) who hands over the initial $10 to start the trust. After that, they walk away. They cannot be a beneficiary, and they cannot influence the trust again — that independence is what makes the trust valid for tax purposes.

Role 2

Trustee

The legal owner of trust assets and the day-to-day decision maker. Owes fiduciary duties to the beneficiaries. Can be an individual or a company (Mitchell Trustee Pty Ltd). Signs contracts, lodges tax returns, makes distributions.

Role 3 — Most Powerful

Appointor

Has the power to hire and fire the trustee. This is where the real control of the trust sits. Whoever holds this role effectively controls the trust, because they can replace any trustee who isn't acting in line with their wishes.

Role 4

Beneficiaries

The people and entities who can receive distributions. Split into Primary, Secondary and Tertiary classes. Important: being listed as a beneficiary doesn't entitle you to anything — the trustee chooses each year who actually gets paid.

How They Connect

Hover or tap a role above to see how it relates to the others.

💡
Why the appointor matters mostThe trustee makes day-to-day decisions, but the appointor can replace the trustee at any time. So in practice, the appointor is the person who controls the trust. When advisers talk about "passing control of the trust to the next generation", what they really mean is changing who holds the appointor role.
Section 03

Types of Trusts in Australia

Australian law recognises several types of trust. They look similar on the surface, but the rules around who can receive what — and how flexibly — are very different.

Most Common
DT

Discretionary (Family) Trust

The trustee has full discretion to decide each year which beneficiaries receive distributions and in what proportions. No beneficiary has a fixed entitlement. This is the structure used by most Australian families and small businesses for asset protection and tax planning.

UT

Unit Trust

Beneficiaries (called "unit holders") own fixed units in the trust, similar to shares in a company. Distributions are made strictly in proportion to units held. Common for unrelated parties investing together in property or business.

FT

Fixed Trust

Beneficiaries have a fixed, defined entitlement to income and capital. The trustee has no discretion. Often used to satisfy specific tax or land-tax rules where flexibility isn't required.

HT

Hybrid Trust

A mix of unit and discretionary features. Some beneficiaries hold fixed units, others receive discretionary distributions. Once popular, but the ATO has tightened the rules and these are used much less today.

TT

Testamentary Trust

Created by a person's Will and only comes into existence on their death. Highly tax-effective for distributions to minor children, who are taxed at adult marginal rates rather than penalty rates.

BT

Bare Trust

The simplest form — the trustee holds an asset purely as nominee for a single beneficiary, with no active duties. Often used in SMSF property purchases under limited recourse borrowing arrangements.

✓
For most individuals and small businessesThe discretionary (family) trust is the default choice. Its flexibility — being able to decide who gets what each year — is exactly what makes it so powerful for tax planning and protecting family wealth.
Section 04

The Three Trustee Structures

When you set up a trust, you choose whether the trustee is a person or a company. Most people start with an individual trustee for simplicity, then upgrade as the trust grows.

① Individual Trustee

Pros: Cheapest and fastest to set up. No ASIC fees. Simple paperwork.

Cons: The individual is personally liable for trust debts. If the trust is sued, the trustee's personal assets are exposed. If the trustee dies, the trust must change trustees, which can be messy.

→

② Corporate Trustee

Pros: The company (e.g., Mitchell Trustee Pty Ltd) takes the legal risk, not James personally. Cleaner governance, easier succession, looks more professional to banks and counterparties.

Cons: Costs ~$500 to set up, ~$310 ASIC annual fee, slightly more paperwork.

🔄
Upgrading without triggering CGTYou can change from an individual trustee to a corporate trustee at any time. Because the trust itself stays the same — only the legal name on the asset registers changes — there's no CGT event and no stamp duty in most states. Your accountant updates the trust deed, registers the new trustee with the ATO, ASIC and the bank, and the trust carries on as before.
Section 05

The Beneficiary Classes Explained

A discretionary trust deed defines who could receive a distribution. The list is usually drafted very widely, in three tiers, plus a default-beneficiary safety net. Below is how it looks for The Mitchell Family Trust.

P

Primary Beneficiaries

James Mitchell personally. The named individual the trust is set up for.

S

Secondary Beneficiaries

Sarah Mitchell (spouse/partner), future children, parents, siblings, and extended family of the primary beneficiary.

T

Tertiary Beneficiaries

Mitchell Investments Pty Ltd (the bucket company), any associated SMSF, related companies and trusts, and registered charities.

D

Default Beneficiary

If no distribution resolution is made by 30 June, income falls automatically to the default beneficiary (usually James). This stops the trust paying penalty tax — but it dumps all the income on one person at the highest rate, so you never want to rely on it.

⚠️
Listed ≠ EntitledBeing named as a beneficiary doesn't guarantee you anything. The trustee decides each financial year — between 1 July and 30 June — who actually receives a distribution and how much. A distant relative listed as a tertiary beneficiary may never see a cent. That flexibility is exactly the point: the trustee can react to each year's circumstances.
Section 06

How Income Flows Through the Trust

A trust doesn't pay tax itself — the income flows through to whoever the trustee chooses, and they pay tax at their own rate. Step through the flow below.

Annual Income Flow — Step by Step

1
Income arrives in the trust
Dividends from shares, business profits, capital gains, interest from term deposits — anything earned by trust-owned assets.
2
Trustee makes a distribution resolution before 30 June
A signed written decision stating exactly who gets what percentage of which type of income. This must be done before midnight 30 June — not after.
3
Income flows to chosen beneficiaries
James, Sarah, Mitchell Investments Pty Ltd, family members — each takes their share into their own tax return at their own marginal rate.
4
Streaming: different income types to different beneficiaries
The trustee can stream franking credits to one beneficiary and capital gains to another, optimising for each person's tax position.
5
Beneficiaries pay tax at their own rates
Franking credits travel with dividend distributions, reducing the receiving beneficiary's tax bill — and may even produce a refund for low-income beneficiaries.
6
⚠️ Miss 30 June and the trustee pays 47% on all undistributed income
If no valid resolution exists, the trustee is taxed on the income at the top marginal rate of 47%. This is the single most expensive admin mistake in trust land.
1 of 6
🚨
The 30 June deadline is non-negotiableThe resolution must be signed and dated on or before 30 June. The ATO does not accept excuses. Missing it triggers tax at 47% on the trust's net income — typically tens of thousands of dollars wasted on a single missed signature.

Section 08

The Bucket Company

A bucket company is a private company set up purely to receive trust distributions and "catch" income that doesn't need to be paid out to individuals. It pays a flat 25% corporate tax — far less than personal top rates — and lets profits compound inside the company.

How it works

Each year, after distributing what James and Sarah personally need, the trustee streams the remaining trust income to Mitchell Investments Pty Ltd. The company pays 25% corporate tax on that distribution. The after-tax money stays inside the company, where it can be reinvested in shares, property or other assets.

When James eventually wants to access the money — perhaps in retirement when his personal income is low — the company pays him a franked dividend. The 25% tax already paid travels with the dividend as a franking credit, so James only tops up to his marginal rate.

47%

James personally (top rate)

25%

Mitchell Investments Pty Ltd

Same dollar of income — almost half the tax inside the bucket company.

🚫
The bucket company and the corporate trustee MUST be different entitiesThe corporate trustee (Mitchell Trustee Pty Ltd) holds the trust assets and bears legal risk. The bucket company (Mitchell Investments Pty Ltd) is a beneficiary that receives distributions and accumulates wealth. If they're the same entity, you've fused the trustee's legal liability with the bucket company's asset base — exactly the wrong outcome. Always two separate companies.
Section 09

Loans From the Trust — Division 7A

A trust can lend money to its beneficiaries. Done correctly, this is a powerful tool for funding investments. Done badly, the ATO treats the loan as an unfranked dividend and taxes it at the top marginal rate.

Why borrow from the trust?

Imagine the trust has $200,000 in retained earnings sitting in Mitchell Investments Pty Ltd. James wants to buy a share portfolio personally but doesn't have the cash. Instead of paying himself a dividend (which triggers personal tax), he borrows the money from the company at the official interest rate. He invests, claims the loan interest as a tax deduction against his investment income, and pays the loan back over up to seven years.

Division 7A in plain English

Division 7A is the ATO rule that stops business owners using their company as a personal piggy bank. It applies whenever a private company (including a bucket company associated with a trust) lends money to a shareholder or their associate. If you don't follow the rules, the entire loan amount is treated as an unfranked dividend in the borrower's tax return.

RequirementWhat it means
Written loan agreementSigned and in place by the company's tax-return lodgement date for the year the loan was made.
Minimum interest rateThe ATO benchmark rate (set each year). For 2024-25 this is around 8.77% — must be reviewed annually.
Maximum term — unsecured7 years from the date of the loan.
Maximum term — secured by registered mortgage25 years, where the loan is secured against real property and meets ATO loan-to-value requirements.
Minimum yearly repaymentsCalculated by formula. Both principal and interest must be paid each year by 30 June.
⚠️
Get the documentation right or pay the priceIf the loan agreement isn't signed in time, or the minimum repayments are missed, the ATO will treat the loan as an unfranked dividend. That means tax at the borrower's marginal rate (up to 47%) on the entire loan balance — with no franking credit to offset it. Always run trust loans through your accountant before any money moves.
Section 10

Adding and Removing Beneficiaries

If your family changes — a new partner, a grandchild, a falling-out with a relative — you can update the beneficiary list with a Deed of Variation. But there are some things you can never reverse, so the smart approach is to draft widely from day one.

What a Deed of Variation does

A Deed of Variation is a short legal document, prepared by a lawyer or specialist trust provider, that amends the original trust deed. Typical uses include adding a new spouse, adding children born after the deed was set up, or excluding someone the family no longer wishes to include. Cost is typically $200 to $500 depending on the provider.

🔒
The foreign person exclusion is irrevocableMost modern trust deeds include a "foreign person exclusion" clause to avoid foreign-purchaser surcharge stamp duty and land tax in NSW, Victoria and other states. Once that clause is in, it cannot be removed without potentially triggering huge retrospective duty bills. So if there's any chance a future beneficiary will be a foreign resident, talk to your adviser before activating the clause.
🎯
Cast the net wide on day oneIt's far cheaper and cleaner to list a broad class of beneficiaries from the start than to keep amending the deed. A well-drafted modern deed already includes spouses, children, grandchildren, parents, siblings, related entities and charities. You'll rarely need a Deed of Variation if it's drafted properly.
Section 11

The Bucket Company + Cashflow Company Structure

A worked example. James runs a 50/50 business with an unrelated partner. The right structure protects James's family wealth, allows tax-efficient income splitting, and keeps the operating risk away from the family's accumulated assets.

Why this layout works

  • Operating risk is ring-fenced. If Cashflow Co is sued, only its assets are at risk — not the trust, not the bucket company, not James personally.
  • The trust owns the shares, not James. If James were sued personally (a car accident, a guarantee gone bad), the trust's shareholding in Cashflow Co is shielded.
  • Profits are tax-streamed. Each year the trustee decides how much profit James needs personally and how much can be parked in the bucket company at 25%.
📜
You must have a shareholders agreementAny joint venture between unrelated parties needs a written shareholders agreement covering decision-making, dispute resolution, exit rights and what happens if one shareholder dies, divorces or wants out. Without it, even close friendships fall apart when money is involved.

Section 13

Common Mistakes to Avoid

Trusts are unforgiving — small admin slips can trigger enormous tax bills. These are the six mistakes accountants see most often.

!

Missing the 30 June distribution deadline

The trustee gets taxed at 47% on every undistributed dollar. No exceptions, no extensions.

!

Mixing trust and personal funds

Paying personal expenses from the trust account, or vice versa. Destroys the legal separation that makes the trust work.

!

Same company as both trustee and bucket company

Fuses operating risk with accumulated wealth. Defeats the entire purpose of the structure.

!

No written agreement for trust loans

The ATO treats the loan as an unfranked dividend taxed at the borrower's marginal rate.

!

Transferring personal assets into the trust without considering CGT

Moving an existing investment into the trust is a sale at market value — capital gains tax is payable immediately.

!

Buying a deed from a cheap or unknown provider

A poorly drafted deed limits your flexibility for decades. Use a reputable specialist provider only.
